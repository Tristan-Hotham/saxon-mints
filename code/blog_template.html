Some of the small ones all but disappeared.

Lydford is a village on the western edge of Dartmoor. Its parish has {{towns.Lydford.pop2021|int}} residents. A thousand years ago it was a mint town: its surviving coins name {{towns.Lydford.moneyers_late_as|words}} different moneyers between the reigns of Æthelred II and Edward the Confessor, and Domesday Book counts {{towns.Lydford.burgesses1086|int}} burgesses there in 1086. Then the coins stop.

Late Anglo-Saxon England had a remarkably organised coinage. After King Edgar's reform, around 973, every silver penny carried the king's name, the moneyer who struck it and the town where it was struck. That makes the coins a kind of census: a list of the places that mattered enough to hold a mint, and, through the number of moneyers, a rough measure of how much each one made. So I asked a simple question. Were the towns that made England's money still the important towns later on?

I gathered {{counts.records_unique|int}} coin records from the Fitzwilliam Museum's database of early medieval coins and coin finds, and kept the {{counts.window_certain_ew|int}} from Edgar's reform to the death of Stephen in 1154 whose mint is a single, certain place in England or Wales.

Seventy mints at the peak

Surviving coins of Æthelred II come from {{mints_per_reign.aethelred|int}} mints. Under Cnut the number peaks at {{mints_per_reign.cnut|int}}, and under Edward the Confessor it is {{mints_per_reign.edwardc|int}}. After the Conquest it dips, to {{mints_per_reign.william1|int}} under William I and {{mints_per_reign.henry1|int}} under Henry I, before Stephen's troubled reign adds new mints such as Castle Rising, Dunwich, Cardiff and Newcastle.

These are mints with surviving coins, so they are a floor rather than a census. Harold II reigned for nine months in 1066, and the database has his coins from {{mints_per_reign.harold2|int}} mints, where Wikipedia's history of the English penny gives {{wiki_context.harold2_mints|int}}.

London, York and Lincoln

The mints were wildly unequal. Under Æthelred II, London coins name {{towns.London.moneyers_aethelred|int}} different moneyers, York {{towns.York.moneyers_aethelred|int}} and Lincoln {{towns.Lincoln.moneyers_aethelred|int}}. Those three hold {{london_york_lincoln_share_aethelred|pct0}} of all the moneyers in the reign. Winchester, the old West Saxon royal centre, has {{towns.Winchester.moneyers_aethelred|int}}, and Stamford {{towns.Stamford.moneyers_aethelred|int}}.

Local money, travelling far

Some of the records are single finds: coins lost one at a time, with a findspot. They are the best guide to money in everyday use. I could place {{circulation.emc_placed|int}} of them on the map. The median coin was found {{circulation.all.median_km|int}} km from the mint that struck it, and {{circulation.all.beyond100|pct0}} had gone more than 100 km.

So money moved. But not at random. If every coin in a purse had been drawn from the national output, the median distance would be about {{circulation.random_mint_median_km|int}} km. And {{circulation.all.nearest_obs|pct0}} of finds were struck at the nearest working mint, {{circulation.all.nearest_ratio|f1}} times what free mixing would give. A penny was still most likely to be lost near home. London's coins spread widest of the big four: only {{circulation.london.within50|pct0}} of London finds lie within 50 km of the city.

Were the big mints still big?

Mostly, yes. {{destiny.top10_in1377|Words}} of the ten biggest mints of Æthelred's reign appear among the {{destiny.list1377_n|int}} largest towns in the poll tax of 1377. London is first in 1086, 1377, 1801 and 2021. Across all the mint towns of that reign, the rank correlation between moneyers then and residents now is {{destiny.spearman_aethelred_2021|f2}}: moderate, but across a thousand years.

The year that breaks the pattern is 1801. By then only {{destiny.top10_in1801|words}} of the ten were in the top 30, and the correlation falls to {{destiny.spearman_aethelred_1801|f2}}. Of the 30 largest towns of 1801, only {{destiny.list1801_mints|int}} had struck coins in my period. None of Manchester, Liverpool, Birmingham, Leeds or Sheffield had a mint in those centuries. Today, {{destiny.top30_2021_mints|int}} of England's 30 largest built-up areas were mint towns. The industrial cities did not grow out of the old network. They grew beside it.

Inside the network there were losers. Winchester, fourth among the mints, was {{towns.Winchester.rank1377|int}}th in 1377 and has {{towns.Winchester.pop2021|int}} people today. Thetford, seventh, with {{towns.Thetford.burgesses1086|int}} burgesses in Domesday, is missing from the 1377 list and has {{towns.Thetford.pop2021|int}} residents. Wallingford had {{towns.Wallingford.moneyers_aethelred|int}} moneyers and now has {{towns.Wallingford.pop2021|int}} people. Bristol went the other way: one moneyer in Æthelred's reign, third largest town in 1377.

The mints that vanished

{{destiny.n_vanished|int}} mint towns have fewer than 5,000 people today. Cadbury's coins were struck at the hillfort of Cadbury Castle; the parish beside it has {{towns.Cadbury.pop2021|int}} residents. Cissbury, on the South Downs, has none. Ilchester had {{towns.Ilchester.moneyers_late_as|int}} moneyers before 1066 and has {{towns.Ilchester.pop2021|int}} residents in its built-up area now; Wilton had {{towns.Wilton.moneyers_late_as|int}} and has {{towns.Wilton.pop2021|int}}. Torksey on the Trent had {{towns.Torksey.burgesses1086|int}} burgesses in 1086 and has a parish of {{towns.Torksey.pop2021|int}}. Dunwich, which Russell's estimate puts at {{towns.Dunwich.pop1086_russell|int}} people in 1086, has a parish of {{towns.Dunwich.pop2021|int}}. Only {{destiny.n_grew|words}} tiny mints have grown past 100,000: Newcastle, Reading, Peterborough and Swansea, all late or marginal mints.

How I did this

I searched the Fitzwilliam Museum's public EMC/SCBI database by each of its {{counts.mint_values_searched|int}} mint values, removed {{counts.duplicates|int}} duplicate records, and counted only coins whose mint is a single, certain place: queried mints, copies and ambiguous ones such as "Bath or Barnstaple" are left out and listed in the data. A mint's size in a reign is the number of different moneyers named on its coins, the usual rough measure of output. Mints and findspots are placed with the Ordnance Survey's OS Open Names gazetteer. The 1086, 1377 and 1801 figures come from Wikipedia's list of English towns by historical population, Domesday burgesses from my Domesday Destiny study, and 2021 populations from Census 2021 small areas summed to built-up areas.

The caveats matter. The database is what has been catalogued, not every coin ever struck, so counts are floors. Moneyer spellings can split one person in two. The 1377 and 1801 lists cover only the largest towns. And I publish only counts and record links, not the museum's descriptions. For more on Viking-age silver, see my Silver and Names study.
